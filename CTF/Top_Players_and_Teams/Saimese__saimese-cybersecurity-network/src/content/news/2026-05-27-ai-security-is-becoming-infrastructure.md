The clearest AI and cybersecurity story in the last 24 hours is not one flashy exploit or one product launch. It is that AI security is hardening into infrastructure. The conversation has moved beyond whether frontier models can find meaningful software flaws. Now the real questions are who can absorb the findings, who can govern model usage, and who can keep critical systems standing while all of that accelerates.

That shift showed up most plainly on May 27 in Europe. Reuters reported that the European Central Bank is pressing euro-zone banks to spend more on cybersecurity because new AI models can expose software weaknesses faster than many institutions can respond. ECB Vice President Luis de Guindos framed the issue as structural, not temporary. That matters. Once banking regulators start treating AI-enabled cyber risk as a recurring budget line rather than a niche technical concern, the market has crossed a line. AI security stops being an innovation project and becomes core operational resilience.

The pressure behind that warning is easy to see in Anthropic’s latest Project Glasswing data, which is still setting the agenda this week. In its May 22 update, Anthropic said Claude Mythos Preview and roughly 50 partners had already found more than 10,000 high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities across critical software. Its coordinated disclosure dashboard said that, as of May 22, Anthropic had disclosed 1,596 vulnerabilities across 281 open-source projects. That is the important nuance: the bottleneck is no longer discovery alone. It is triage, disclosure, maintainer capacity, and patch deployment. AI is speeding up the front of the pipeline faster than the rest of the ecosystem is ready to catch up.

What happens next is enterprise plumbing, and that is where another meaningful development landed this week. SecurityWeek reported that Anthropic expanded Claude with 28 security and compliance integrations spanning SIEM, identity, data loss prevention, AI observability, and e-discovery platforms. That may sound less dramatic than a new exploit benchmark, but it is probably more important for adoption. Security teams do not just want powerful models; they want logs, policy enforcement, alerting, governance, and a way to fit AI into the same control stack they already use for the rest of the business. In other words, the vendors are now building the rails that let companies use powerful models without treating them as a blind spot.

Put those pieces together and the real headline is straightforward. AI security is becoming infrastructure. Regulators are treating it as systemic risk. Model developers are building disclosure pipelines at unprecedented scale. Enterprise vendors are wiring those models into compliance and monitoring stacks. The center of gravity is shifting from experimentation to operations.

That shift is also why the tone of this week’s coverage feels different from the earlier AI-security cycle. The novelty is fading, and the integration work is taking over. Security leaders now have to assume that model-assisted bug discovery will keep accelerating, that disclosures will arrive in larger batches, and that governance expectations from boards and regulators will keep rising. The hard part is no longer proving that AI matters. The hard part is building the response capacity to live with it.

The organizations that adapt fastest will not just be the ones with the best models. They will be the ones that can turn AI-speed discovery into AI-governed, human-executable defense.
